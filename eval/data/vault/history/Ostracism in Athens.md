# Ostracism in Athens

Once a year the Athenian Assembly voted on whether to hold an ostracism. If yes, citizens gathered in the Agora and each scratched a name on a broken pottery shard, an *ostrakon* (plural ostraka). If at least 6,000 votes were cast, the man named most often was exiled for ten years, but kept his property and citizenship.

It was a safety valve of Athenian democracy against would-be tyrants, introduced in Cleisthenes' reforms. In practice it became a weapon in factional politics: Themistocles, Aristides "the Just" and Cimon were all ostracised.

Archaeologists have found over 11,000 ostraka shards near the Agora and Kerameikos, some written in the same handwriting, which suggests organised groups handed out pre-written shards to voters.

The last ostracism, around 417 BC during the Peloponnesian War, backfired when rivals Nicias and Alcibiades teamed up to get the demagogue Hyperbolus exiled instead, and the practice fell out of use.
